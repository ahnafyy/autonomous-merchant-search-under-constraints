\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We set out to answer a question engineers building shopping agents actually face:
when is an adaptive stopping rule worth more than a fixed budget?

The answer has three parts. Offers in the live corpus are genuinely ephemeral, so
stopping is a real decision rather than a formality. An adaptive reservation-price
rule does beat the strongest tuned fixed rule on held-out live panels, with the whole
paired interval below zero and no budget violations. And the margin is small, for a
reason that turns out to be the most useful output of the work: when merchants charge
nearly the same price, or when the budget is too tight to act on what a query
reveals, there is nothing for an optimizer to win, and in the tight-budget case it
can lose.

That last part is why we report a decision table rather than a headline improvement.
A method that always helps is usually a method whose baselines were not tried
seriously. Mapping where a technique stops paying for itself is more useful to
someone shipping an agent than another point estimate, and it is the part of this
work most likely to survive contact with a different corpus.

The narrowness of the benchmark is itself a finding. Cross-merchant product overlap
in this ecosystem is thin, and half of the episodes we could build have no price
spread to exploit. Any claim that adaptive search yields large savings for autonomous
shoppers should be checked against how often the same product is actually purchasable
in more than one place at meaningfully different prices.

We release the optimizer, the permit ledger, the replay harness, the collection
scripts, and the dated snapshots, so the measurement can be repeated on a wider corpus
or at a longer horizon than we could reach.
