\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

We report the live-panel comparison first, then the size of the effect, then the
sweep that explains it. Every value below is read from generated artifacts.

\subsection{Adaptive stopping beats the best fixed rule}

Eight policies were replayed against the same frozen panels, sharing a merchant
arrival order and a per-episode failure penalty. The fixed rules were tuned on
calibration episodes only, so they are represented at their best rather than as
strawmen.

The \texttt{never accept} row is a control. It is scored under the same failure
penalty as every other policy, and its very large loss confirms that terminal failure
is not being treated as a free outcome that a lazy policy could exploit.

\subsection{The effect is real and small}

The same result stated as a fraction of what is being spent: the improvement is a
fraction of one percent of purchase cost. We consider the size to be as much a result
as the sign, and two features of the corpus explain it.

First, dispersion. The median episode has a cross-merchant price ratio of
\ResultMedianPriceDispersionRatio{}, so in half of all episodes the merchants charge
essentially the same price and every policy makes the same purchase. Those episodes
contribute no difference in either direction.

Second, horizon. The median episode has \ResultMedianMerchantCount{} merchants. With
two merchants, an optimal rule and a one-step lookahead are the same rule, which is
why \texttt{adaptive stopping} and \texttt{myopic voi} are indistinguishable in
Table~\ref{tab:arms}. This result therefore does not separate those two policies, and
we do not claim that it does. It shows that reasoning about the continuation beats
fixed rules; it does not show that the full dynamic program is needed to do so.

\subsection{What the hand-computable rule is worth, and what the 37\% rule costs}

The live corpus cannot separate these rules: with \ResultMedianMerchantCount{}
merchants per episode there is almost no exploration to get wrong. We therefore
compare them on the calibrated generator at
\ResultRuleComparisonMerchantCount{} merchants, where exploration strategy matters.
\emph{These are simulation results.}

Two things stand out.

\paragraph{The closed form is worth almost as much as the solver.}
Equation~\eqref{eq:closedform} captures
\ResultClosedFormSavingsCapturedPercent\% of the achievable saving against the exact
solver's \ResultSolverSavingsCapturedPercent\%, using nothing but the expected price
range and a query count. It also matches the solver's reservation price to within
\ResultClosedFormMaxRelativeErrorPercent\% across the horizons we checked, the
residual being price-grid discretization (claim \texttt{CLOSED-FORM-RULE-001}). For an
engineer, the dynamic program is not the deliverable; this is.

We checked the obvious objection, that the acceptance fractions assume uniform
prices. Substituting lognormal, bimodal, and one-cheap-merchant price laws leaves the
rule capturing a large majority of the achievable saving in every case, so the rule
degrades gracefully when its own assumption is wrong.

\paragraph{The 37\% rule costs about half the available saving.}
The secretary rule captures only \ResultSecretarySavingsCapturedPercent\% while
spending more queries than any other online arm. It searches harder and does worse. The reason is
structural rather than incidental: it is hunting for the single cheapest merchant,
and pays for record-chasing with both queries and purchase failures, when the
objective only ever rewarded the expected price paid (claim
\texttt{SECRETARY-RULE-001}).

This is not a counterexample to the secretary result, and the mismatch it illustrates
is already known~\citep{bearden2006secretary,palley2014sequential}. What we add is its
size in this setting. If an agent has genuinely no idea what prices look like, an
ordinal rule remains the right tool; the moment it has even a rough price range,
\eqref{eq:closedform} dominates.

\subsection{When the rule pays for itself}

To find out where adaptive stopping matters, we sweep conditions the live corpus does
not span, using a generator calibrated to the measured delisting rate, price drift,
and dispersion. \emph{These are simulation results.} They inherit the calibration but
not the authority of the live panels, and merchant prices are drawn independently,
which real merchants may not be.

Three regions appear, and \ResultSweepCellsFavoringAdaptive{} of
\ResultSweepCells{} cells favour the adaptive rule.

\paragraph{Use adaptive stopping when all three hold.}
Prices genuinely differ across merchants, at a spread of at least
\ResultAdvantageMinDispersionRatio{}$\times$; the budget allows querying most of the
merchants, so the agent can act on what it learns; and more than one alternative
remains reachable. In the best cell the improvement reaches
\ResultAdvantageMaxRelativeImprovement{} of purchase cost.

\paragraph{Do not bother when prices are flat.}
At a spread of \ResultNoAdvantageDispersionAtOrBelow{}$\times$ or below, no cell
favours adaptive stopping at any merchant count or budget. This is not a weak effect
that more data would resolve; when every merchant charges the same price, there is
nothing to optimize, and a constant rule is the correct engineering choice.

\paragraph{Do not use it when the budget is tight.}
In \ResultSweepCellsFavoringFixed{} cells the fixed rule wins outright, with the
adaptive rule significantly worse. These are cells with a low budget fraction and
modest dispersion: the rule declines an acceptable offer in the hope of something
better, then runs out of queries before it can act. Information the agent cannot
afford to use is worse than no information, because it still changes the decision
(claim \texttt{NO-ADVANTAGE-REGION-001}).
