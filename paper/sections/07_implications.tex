\section{Implications for Shopping Agents}
\label{sec:implications}

The practical rule is simple: search again only when its expected saving exceeds its
cost. An agent should estimate that value from the price spread it sees, the sellers
still reachable, and the resources another inspection consumes. If it can return to
an earlier offer at the observed price, retain the best card in state; if it must
revalidate or faces expiry risk, price that transition as part of the next search.

An aggregator lowers search cost inside its observed product--seller graph. That is
precisely where a decision rule can recognize that another reveal is cheap enough to
earn its keep. A direct merchant lookup, a different aggregator, or a revalidation
call is a higher-cost expansion step. Agents should use the same calculation to
decide whether to take it, rather than applying one fixed search depth everywhere.

Our held-out result adds an operational guardrail: adaptive search can beat search-all
when inspection cost is material because it stops before low-value remaining reveals.
Retain a best offer, keep calibration separate from evaluation, and include inspection
cost in total acquisition cost. At near-zero cost, search-all is not reliably worse;
at exactly zero cost it is reliably better. As the cost rises, adaptation earns its
complexity by stopping selectively.
