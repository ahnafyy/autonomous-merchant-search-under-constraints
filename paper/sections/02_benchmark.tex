\section{The Benchmark: Measuring a Live Merchant Ecosystem}
\label{sec:benchmark}

Most work on stopping problems assumes a price distribution. We would rather measure
one. This section describes the corpus, what it does and does not represent, and the
two properties that make it usable as a benchmark.

\subsection{Collection}

Merchants implementing the Universal Commerce Protocol publish a discovery document
and expose a read-only catalog search operation. We probed every discoverable such
merchant and paginated each catalog, reaching
\ResultOverlapMerchantsScanned{} merchants. Of the products offered by more than
one independent seller in a single currency,
\ResultOverlapEpisodeEligibleProducts{} qualify as potential episodes.

The earlier of our two dated snapshots, used for calibration, covers a narrower set
of \ResultMerchantsTargeted{} merchants and \ResultCatalogRows{} listings; the
replayed episodes are drawn from that intersection. Section~\ref{sec:limitations}
records what that costs.

Only read operations were used; nothing was added to a cart and no purchase was
attempted. Merchant eligibility follows a documented permission ladder, and the
inventory used here is labelled \emph{declared capability}: the merchant advertises
the operation we exercised. That is a recorded modelling assumption, not a
case-by-case terms-of-service review, and it is a limitation rather than a warrant.
The crawler identifies itself with a contact address, stops immediately on any
refusal status, and never contacts a refusing merchant again.

\subsection{The instrument does not add noise}

Before trusting any difference between two dates, we checked the scanner against
itself. Two full scans of the same merchants minutes apart returned byte-identical
listings and prices. Pagination is deterministic and result ordering is stable, so
any difference observed between snapshots is drift in the world.

This check matters more than it might appear. An early comparison suggested roughly
half of all listings vanished within a month; it was an artifact of comparing a
shallow scan against a deep one. Restricting the comparison to merchants whose
catalogs were paginated \emph{in full} on both dates removes the artifact, and we
report only that restricted figure.

\subsection{Why offers are not recallable}

The benchmark treats an offer as accept-now-or-lose-it. That assumption is often
defended by arguing that prices move, which invites the objection that they move
slowly: the online price-rigidity literature consistently finds long spells of fixed
prices, and finds online prices \emph{stickier} than earlier estimates
suggested~\citep{cavallo2018scraped,gorodnichenko2018jeea,gorodnichenko2017basic}.
Our own instrument agrees at short horizons, since two scans minutes apart are
byte-identical.

We therefore do not rest the assumption on price movement. We rest it on the
protocol. The UCP catalog specification states that catalog responses ``are not
transactional commitments'' and ``SHOULD NOT be reused across sessions without
re-validation''~\citep{ucp2026catalog}. A checkout session carries an explicit
expiry, defaulting to six hours~\citep{ucp2026checkout}, and the corresponding
payment-authorisation protocol issues mandates with a one-hour validity window in its
normative example~\citep{ap22026checkout}.

An agent that observes a catalog price and does not convert it within the session
thus has no protocol-guaranteed claim on that price, whether or not the number
changed. No-recall holds by construction, and the episode horizon is bounded by the
quote lifetime rather than by any drift rate we measure.

Two caveats. The protocols differ: the checkout specification we cite defines an
expiry field, while at least one competing agentic-commerce specification does not,
so this argument is not uniform across the ecosystem. And a specification bounds what
an agent may \emph{rely} on, not what merchants do in practice; a merchant may well
honour a stale price. The assumption is therefore conservative rather than exact.

\subsection{Offers move}

Across \ResultTrackedListings{} listings tracked on fully paginated merchants,
\ResultDelistingRate{} were delisted between the two dates and
\ResultPriceChangeRate{} of the survivors changed price, with a median relative move
of \ResultMedianRelativePriceChange{} among those that moved. Prices are sticky but
not static, and disappearance is the more common event. An agent that defers a
purchase is therefore taking a real risk, which is the premise the rest of the paper
depends on.

\subsection{Episodes}

An episode is one exact SKU offered by at least two independent merchants in a single
currency. The earlier snapshot supplies merchant price and availability forecasts;
the later snapshot is frozen as the held-out panel that every policy is replayed
against. Because the split is chronological, no policy can see its own evaluation
data.

Two filters do most of the work and both cost us episodes. Cross-currency listings
are dropped, since comparing a euro price to a dollar price requires an exchange-rate
assumption we prefer not to make; this removes the majority of candidate SKUs. A SKU
missing from the later snapshot counts as delisted only when that merchant was
paginated in full, and otherwise the merchant is dropped from the episode rather than
recorded as a stockout. What survives is \ResultEpisodeCount{} episodes, of which
\ResultHeldOutEpisodeCount{} are held out for evaluation.

\subsection{What this corpus is not}

The resulting benchmark is narrow in a way worth stating plainly. The median replayed
episode has \ResultMedianMerchantCount{} merchants and a price ratio of
\ResultMedianPriceDispersionRatio{}, meaning that in half of all episodes the
merchants charge nearly the same price and no policy can distinguish itself.

That thinness is a property of the ecosystem, not of our pipeline, and we can now
quantify it. Across \ResultOverlapMerchantsScanned{} merchants,
\ResultOverlapEpisodeEligibleProducts{} products are sold by two or more independent
sellers, but only \ResultOverlapProductsThreeOrMore{} reach three or more and
\ResultOverlapProductsFiveOrMore{} reach five, with the widest observed overlap being
\ResultOverlapWidestMerchantCount{} merchants (claim
\texttt{OVERLAP-SPARSITY-001}). Since an optimal rule and a one-step lookahead
coincide when a single alternative remains, this bounds what any stopping policy can
demonstrate on live data, and it is the reason
Section~\ref{sec:results} reports the two as indistinguishable.

Overlap is detected by exact SKU equality, so the same product listed under different
identifiers at two merchants counts as two products. The measured overlap is a lower
bound.
